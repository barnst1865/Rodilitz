Morris, Sylvia and the Hausmans

Theodore’s parents in East New York, and Sylvia’s mother, who came by way of Havana in 1925.

Morris

Morris Rodilitz, the eldest son, was born Motel on 17 April 1890. He was the second of the family to cross, landing at Ellis Island from the Breslau on 16 December 1907, seven months after his father. The list describes an 18-year-old who could read and write, from Czerniany in Grodno province, where his mother still lived; his father had paid his passage, and he arrived with $2.1

Czerniany is probably the village of Chernyany (Charniany), now in Malaryta district of Belarus’s Brest region. Before 1914 it was in Brest district of Grodno province, about 27 km south-south-west of Kobrin and 35 km from Brest. It was a village with a handful of Jewish families, about a hundred people before 1941, not a market town.2

Morris declared his intention to become a citizen on 15 November 1913, the same day as his brother Harry, and was naturalized in Brooklyn in 1921. Both brothers gave their birthplace only as “Grodno, Russia”; their certificates of arrival confirm the 1907 and 1910 passenger lists. He worked as a salesman in 1915, in the “novelty” trade in 1930, and as a stock clerk in 1940.3

Morris and Sylvia

On 7 November 1915 Morris married Sivia (Sylvia) Hausman at 41 Malta Street, on the same street as his family’s home at 115 Malta Street. She was 23, living at 312 Palmetto Street, and, like Morris, gave her birthplace as Grodno, Russia. Her parents were Joseph Hausman and Rose Shapiro.4

Sylvia later gave 1910 as the year she came to America. Her crossing has not been found, despite searches under many spellings; she may have travelled under a different name, or, like her mother and sister later, by way of Cuba.5

Morris and Sylvia’s first years were spent with his family: in 1920 they and their two children were a second household at 188 Malta Street. By 1925 they had their own home at 537 Georgia Avenue, where they were still living in 1940. They had four children:

  • Millie (Mildred), born about 1917;
  • Theodore, born on 25 April 1919 in Brooklyn;
  • a son, born on 6 August 1924, who lived four days and was buried at Mount Lebanon Cemetery;
  • Blanche, born about 1927.6

Sylvia died on 14 December 1952, and Morris on 6 January 1957, aged 66. They are buried at New Montefiore Cemetery, West Babylon, Long Island, in a family plot that Jacob and Mary’s children named for their parents: the “Jacob & Miriam Rose Rodolitz” family circle. Morris’s brothers Meyer (1950), Harry (1967) and Louis (1970) lie there too.7

Rose Hausman, by way of Havana

Sylvia’s mother was very probably the Rose (Reise, Rejza) Hausman who came to New York on 12 May 1925 aboard the Ebro from Havana; her name, age, widowhood and timing all fit. The list calls her a widow of 60, born at Brassc, which is probably Brześć, the Polish name for Brest-Litovsk. She left a daughter, Fega, in Havana, at San Ignacio 57, and was going to another daughter, G. Weisberg, at 636 East 135th Street in the Bronx. Officers held her for two days as “likely to become a public charge,” a rule used especially against elderly widows. Three weeks later the state census found her in Morris and Sylvia’s home at 537 Georgia Avenue.8

Rose’s route was typical of its time. The quota laws of 1921 and 1924 sharply limited immigration from eastern Europe, and Cuba became a waystation for Jews hoping to reach the United States. How Rose qualified to enter is a question for her immigration file. Her husband Joseph Hausman had died before 1925, probably in Europe or Cuba. Rose died in the Bronx on 15 November 1932, aged 67; her death certificate names her parents as William Sapir(?) and Hannah Katz, Sapir being a form of Shapiro.8

Theodore

Theodore Rodilitz was born in Brooklyn on 25 April 1919 and grew up at 537 Georgia Avenue. In 1940 he was single, living with his parents and working as an assistant manager. When he registered for the draft that autumn, aged 21, he worked for the Comet Manufacturing Company at 656 Broadway, Manhattan, and named his mother, Mrs. Sylvia Rodilitz, as the person who would always know his address.9 He enlisted in the Army in April 1941 and was a soldier when, in September 1943, he married for the first time, at the Twin Cantors Temple on Eastern Parkway; that marriage was annulled in December 1946.10
