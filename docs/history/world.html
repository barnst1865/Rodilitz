Part one

The world they left

The Pale of Settlement, the home towns, why the families left and how they travelled.

In this chapter
  1. The home towns
  2. Why they left, and when
  3. The journey

Every branch of this family came from the Pale of Settlement, the band of western provinces of the Russian Empire, from the Baltic to the Black Sea, where most Jews were required by law to live. In 1897 the Russian census counted about 4.9 million Jews in the Pale. They were about one in nine of its people, but most of them lived in towns and market towns (shtetlekh), where they were often the majority, and almost all of them spoke Yiddish.1

Grodno province, the Rodilitzes’ home, lay in the north of the Pale. In 1897 its Kobrin district had 23,080 Jews and Brest district 45,902, and in the town of Kobrin itself Jews were about 69% of the population. Podolia, home of the Saslows and Zaslows, lay in the south. Tulchin had 10,055 Jews, 62% of the town; Obodovka, where Benjamin Zaslow was born, was much smaller, with about 1,675 Jews, a fifth of its people.2

The law hemmed these communities in. After the pogroms of 1881–82, the government’s “Temporary Rules” of May 1882, known as the May Laws, barred Jews from settling anew in the countryside. In Kobrin a decree of the same year also stopped Jews from leasing farms and rural buildings. In 1897 the state took over the liquor trade, which had supported many Jewish families, and Kobrin’s local history dates the start of heavy emigration, “especially to America,” to that year. Brest burned in two great fires, in 1895 and 1901, and a Brest memorial book records “a great exodus from the city” after the second.3

Jacob Rodilitz was a Hebrew teacher (melamed), one of the commonest and worst-paid occupations in this world. A teacher usually ran a heder, a one-room religious school for small boys, often in his own home, and his family often needed other work to make ends meet.4

Why they left, and when

No record found so far gives any of these emigrants’ own reasons for leaving, so what follows is general history, set against the dates on which each of them left. Historians used to explain the great Jewish emigration chiefly by pogroms. More recent work, especially by Gur Alroey, gives more weight to poverty, to letters from relatives already abroad, and to the spread of information about the journey.5

The Saslows left in 1892–93. In 1891 about 30,000 Jews were expelled from Moscow into the already crowded Pale, and in 1892 cholera spread across the empire and reached Hamburg. Jewish immigration from Russia to the United States reached 76,417 in 1892, the highest figure before 1900.6

Benjamin Zaslow left in March 1904, aged 19, just after Japan attacked Port Arthur and the Russo-Japanese War began. Young men were called up in the year they turned 21, so his own call-up would have come in 1906; the wartime pressure of 1904–05 fell first on reservists, men who had already served. Jewish immigration from Russia rose from 47,689 in 1903 to 77,544 in 1904.7 Whether the war or the draft had anything to do with his leaving is unknown.

The Rodilitzes left between 1907 and 1913, when emigration had become routine, carried by letters home and tickets bought in America and sent back to Europe. No pogrom has been found in Kobrin between 1881 and 1914, nor in Tulchin or Obodovka in 1903–06. Brest saw violence in 1905, and Białystok, in the same province, a pogrom in 1906 in which about 80 Jews were killed. Tulchin and Obodovka suffered their worst pogroms later, in 1919.8

The journey

To leave legally, a Russian subject needed a foreign passport, which was slow and costly to obtain. Perhaps three quarters of Jewish emigrants crossed the border without one, with the help of agents and smugglers. From 1893–94 the German shipping lines ran control stations along the Prussian border, where emigrants bound for Hamburg and Bremen were bathed, examined for disease, and checked for money and a ticket. Alroey puts the full cost of getting one adult from the Pale to America at about 165–215 roubles, at a time when a working family earned perhaps 500–600 roubles a year. That is why families so often went one or two at a time.9

The family used every main route. Morris sailed from Bremen; Harry, and later Mary with the youngest children, from Hamburg; Jacob, Meyer and Benny with the Holland-America Line from Rotterdam. The Saslows went the “indirect” way, from Hamburg to Britain and on from Glasgow, and the Zaslows from Liverpool. Steerage passengers landing at New York went through Ellis Island, opened in 1892, where officers checked them against the ship’s list and could hold anyone “likely to become a public charge.” Philadelphia’s arrivals landed at the Washington Avenue station on Pier 53.10
