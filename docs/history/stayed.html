Appendix B

Those who stayed behind, 1914–1945

The First World War, the interwar years and the Holocaust in the home towns.

Every one of these families left Europe between 1892 and 1925. Relatives who stayed lived through the First World War, the redrawing of borders, and the Holocaust. No individual relative has yet been traced: searches of Yad Vashem’s database of Holocaust victims under about 28 spellings of Rodilitz, and under Zaslavsky, Saslow and Hausman in the home towns, found no one who can be linked to the family. The Lesnoff name, found after those searches, has not yet been checked. Memorial books do list Prozansky families killed in Antopol, Khomsk and Kamen-Kashirsky, all within about 50 km of Divin, but none is known to be Mary’s.1

The First World War. In August 1915 the retreating Russian army abandoned Kovel and Brest-Litovsk and ordered the Jews of the front zone east; Brest’s Jews were expelled on 1 August 1915. The peace between Germany and Soviet Russia was signed at Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918.

Between the wars. Brest, Kobrin, Divin, Kovel and Maciejów became part of Poland in 1919–21, after fighting around Kobrin and Kovel in September 1920. Tulchin and Obodovka stayed in Soviet Ukraine. In 1919, during the civil war, pogroms killed about 170 Jews in Tulchin and between 150 and 300 in Obodovka.

The Holocaust in the Kobrin district. Germany invaded on 22 June 1941 and reached Kobrin the next day. About 8,000 Jews were confined in two ghettos. One was destroyed in the summer of 1942, when about 1,800 people were shot at Bronnaya Gora; the other on 14–15 October 1942, when about 4,000–4,500 were shot beside the Kobrin–Divin road. About 6,900 Kobrin Jews were murdered. Divin’s Jews were confined in a ghetto and killed in 1942, and Czerniany’s hundred or so Jews were shot in the spring or summer of that year.

Brest. Brest had about 21,500 Jews in 1936. About 5,000 men were shot in the summer of 1941. The ghetto, set up in December 1941 with about 18,000 people, was destroyed on 15 October 1942, most of its people being killed at Bronnaya Gora. Only a handful of Jews, perhaps ten to twenty, were alive in the city when it was liberated in July 1944.

Kovel and Maciejów. Kovel’s ghetto was set up in May 1942 and destroyed between June and October 1942. In Maciejów, which had about 2,600 Jews in 1941, about 1,000 were shot in the quarry in August 1941, and the ghetto was destroyed in the late summer of 1942.

Tulchin and Obodovka. Southern Podolia came under Romanian rule as part of “Transnistria.” Tulchin’s Jews were confined in September 1941 and about 3,000 were sent to the camp at Pechora, where most died. Obodovka received about 10,000–11,000 Jews deported from Bessarabia and Bukovina in November 1941; thousands died there of typhus and hunger. What happened to Obodovka’s own few hundred Jews is not recorded separately.

The families that left between 1892 and 1925 escaped all of this. Any relatives who stayed in Kobrin, Divin or Brest would almost certainly have been killed in 1942.2
